# Patient-controlled sedation for subcutaneous venous port implantation: a short literature review

**Quality improvement project — living document**  
Search date: August 2026. This is a targeted narrative review to inform local implementation, not a systematic review.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This review summarises the evidence most relevant to a small quality improvement (QI) project that would *offer* patient-controlled sedation (PCS) with propofol and alfentanil as an adjunct to local anaesthesia (LA) during implantation of a subcutaneous venous port (SVP; also called a totally implantable venous access device, TIVAD).

The practical question is not “does PCS always reduce pain?” The PACSPI programme has already answered that for this procedure. The practical question is whether PCS can be introduced as a *patient-centred option* with acceptable safety, workflow, and operator conditions.

## 2. Clinical context

Sweden records on the order of 70 000 new cancer diagnoses a year,[1] and about 8 000 SVPs are implanted annually according to the Swedish Perioperative Registry, making the procedure one of the most common operations in that register.[2] The patients are typically adults with cancer, often older and with substantial comorbidity. The procedure is minor surgery: ultrasound-guided venous puncture, catheter tunnelling, and a subcutaneous pocket for the port. It is usually done as a day case.

There is no consensus on peri-procedural analgesia or sedation. Practice ranges from LA alone, through clinician-controlled sedation (CCS), to general anaesthesia, and is often driven by local tradition rather than comparative evidence.[3,4] Observational work from a Swedish county hospital found that about one in four patients report substantial pain or discomfort when SVP implantation is performed under LA alone.[5] Qualitative and survey studies show that a difficult first implant can make patients refuse a later procedure under LA.[6]

The implication for QI is straightforward: a sizeable minority of patients have a poor experience under LA alone, but most do not. A one-size-fits-all policy of either “always sedate” or “never sedate” will overtreat some patients and undertreat others.

## 3. What patient-controlled sedation is

PCS was described in the early 1990s as the sedative analogue of patient-controlled analgesia: the patient triggers small intravenous boluses from a programmed pump, with or without a lockout interval.[7,8] Propofol is the usual drug because of rapid onset, short context-sensitive half-time, and limited postoperative amnesia compared with midazolam.[8,9] Opioids (alfentanil, remifentanil, fentanyl) are sometimes added when the procedure is painful.

The safety idea is behavioural as well as pharmacological. Once the patient is too sedated to press the button, no further boluses are delivered, so the system tends to limit oversedation — provided bolus size, infusion rate, and lockout are sensible, and someone competent is watching the airway.[9,10]

## 4. Evidence from other procedures

PCS has been studied mainly in dentistry, gastrointestinal endoscopy, bronchoscopy, gynaecological day surgery, and burn care.

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis compared propofol PCS with propofol CCS across 13 randomised trials (1 103 patients). PCS did not change the risk of oxygen desaturation, but it reduced rescue interventions for sedation-related adverse events (risk ratio 0.45, 98% CI 0.25–0.81) and appeared to reduce oversedation. Propofol dose, operator satisfaction, and patient satisfaction were similar. The GRADE quality of evidence was low to very low.[10]

A 2026 scoping review of 110 studies (6 665 patients) found PCS used most often for dental procedures and colonoscopy, usually with propofol or midazolam, mostly in ASA 1–2 patients younger than 70 years. Patient satisfaction was consistently high. Severe adverse events were uncommon. A few studies suggested lower cost than clinician-administered sedation.[11]

Swedish work is particularly relevant. Nilsson’s thesis and subsequent trials showed that propofol PCS is feasible for ERCP, bronchoscopy, burn care, and gynaecological surgery; that patients often prefer it to anaesthetist-controlled sedation; and that adding alfentanil can improve surgical access at the cost of more respiratory events.[9] A hospital-perspective cost analysis found propofol PCS with a bedside nurse anaesthetist cheaper than midazolam sedation for ERCP and bronchoscopy, largely because fewer procedures were aborted and hospital stay was shorter.[12]

Taken together, PCS is a mature technique for short procedures. It is not risk-free, especially when an opioid is added, and it is not automatically better than skilled CCS. Its distinctive value is *titration to the individual patient’s wish to be sedated*, with a built-in brake against oversedation.

## 5. Evidence specific to SVP implantation

### 5.1 PACSPI 1 — feasibility

Seifert and colleagues tested propofol–alfentanil PCS plus LA in 40 patients with cancer undergoing SVP implantation at Ryhov County Hospital.[3] The pump (Syramed μSP6000) delivered 0.5 mL boluses (propofol 4.5 mg + alfentanil 25 µg) over 8 seconds with no lockout (maximum seven boluses per minute). A nurse anaesthetist monitored the patient. All patients received 2 L/min oxygen via a capnography nasal cannula.

Main findings:

- 80% reported maximum pain ≤ 3 on an 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS); median pain 1 (range 0–6).
- Satisfaction with pain management and care was 10/10 (median).
- Patients rated “how important is sedation?” at 9.5 and “how important is it to be in control?” at 8.5.
- Median propofol 66 mg and alfentanil 0.37 mg, with very wide ranges (propofol 0–243 mg).
- Bradypnoea in 4/40 (10%), all responding to verbal stimulus; no hypoxia, chin lift, or mask ventilation.
- Rescue sedation in 1/40 (2.5%).
- More than a third of eligible patients declined, often because they judged sedation unnecessary or wanted to drive home.

The authors concluded that the technique was feasible and well accepted, and that a randomised trial was needed.

### 5.2 PACSPI 2 — randomised trial

PACSPI 2 was an open-label, two-centre RCT at Ryhov and Linköping (n = 340 randomised, 334 analysed) comparing LA alone with LA + the same PCS regimen, slightly tightened to a 10-second bolus (maximum six boluses per minute).[4] Primary outcome: maximum intraoperative pain. The trial was powered to detect a 50% relative reduction in the proportion with NRS ≥ 4, assuming a 25% event rate under LA.

Main findings:

| Result | LA | LA + PCS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Median max pain (NRS) | 2 (IQR 0–3) | 2 (IQR 0–3) |
| Pain NRS ≥ 4 | 22.2% | 22.9% |
| Overall satisfaction | 10 (10–10) | 10 (10–10) |
| Perfect operating conditions | 82.0% | 96.4% |
| Rescue sedation | 8.9% | 0.6% |
| Procedure time (min) | 31.5 | 30.0 |
| Recovery observation (min) | 17 | 16 |
| Median propofol / alfentanil | — | 54 mg / 0.3 mg |
| Hypoxia or obstructed airway | 0 | 2/166 (1.2%) |
| Bradypnoea | 0 | 4/166 (2.4%) |
| Mask ventilation or conversion to GA | 0 | 0 |

Pain and satisfaction did not differ. Operating conditions were better with PCS, and almost one in ten LA patients needed unplanned rescue sedation. Respiratory events with PCS were uncommon, mild, and manageable, but they were not zero. Self-administered volumes ranged from 0 to 23 mL, confirming large inter-individual demand.

The authors’ conclusion is the right one for implementation: **routine PCS should not be used to reduce pain**. **PCS may reasonably be offered to patients who want additional sedation.** The accompanying editorial asked for more work on patient experience and safety before widespread adoption.[4]

### 5.3 Related access procedures

A small interventional-radiology RCT of midazolam–fentanyl PCS versus radiologist-controlled sedation for tunnelled central lines (n = 40) found PCS non-inferior for dose, sedation, and pain, with high satisfaction, but used longer-acting drugs and a two-hour recovery stay.[13] Remifentanil infusion plus propofol TCI for insertion or removal of long-term venous access provided comparable analgesia across dose rates, with more respiratory events at higher opioid rates, and mixed insertion with removal in the same trial.[14] Neither study compared an option against LA alone, so they do not overturn PACSPI 2.

## 6. Safety, staffing, and guidelines

European procedural sedation guidelines (ESA/EBA 2018) require risk assessment, a dedicated sedation practitioner with airway competence, pulse oximetry, blood pressure, ECG as indicated, and defined discharge criteria. Propofol plus an opioid is synergistic for both effect and respiratory depression and needs titration, not a recipe.[15] Swedish SFAI recommendations on procedural propofol sedation, and regional SOPs that implement them, likewise specify fasting, monitoring, competency, and emergency preparedness.[16]

PACSPI 1 and 2 were done in an operating-room setting by anaesthesiologists, with a nurse anaesthetist dedicated to monitoring, supplemental oxygen, and capnography. That is the safety envelope this QI project should copy. The 1.2% rate of hypoxia or airway obstruction in PACSPI 2 is low, but the trial was not powered for rare catastrophic events. Implementation therefore needs explicit stop criteria, emergency equipment, and a rule that only the patient presses the button.

Alfentanil is the main safety modifier. Nilsson found more apnoea when alfentanil was added to propofol PCS for gynaecological surgery.[9] PACSPI kept the alfentanil bolus small (25 µg) and still saw bradypnoea. A local SOP must treat verbal prompting, chin lift, and increased oxygen as expected rescue steps, not as surprises.

## 7. Patient-centredness, not protocol-centredness

Three observations from PACSPI should drive the QI design.

1. **Demand varies.** Some patients press the button a lot, some not at all. More than a third of screened patients in PACSPI 1 declined because they did not want sedation. In PACSPI 2, more than a hundred declined because they *did* want the department’s usual analgosedation. Population averages hide this split.

2. **Pain is the wrong primary target for implementation.** Median NRS was 2 in both groups. Satisfaction was already at the ceiling. What differed was control, operating conditions, and unplanned rescue.

3. **Patients who receive PCS value control.** In PACSPI 2, those allocated to PCS rated “importance of getting a sedative” and “importance of being in control” much higher than the LA group — a post-experience difference consistent with PACSPI 1, where control itself scored 8.5/10.

The right implementation model is therefore **shared decision-making**: eligible patients choose LA alone or LA + PCS after a short, honest explanation that PCS is for comfort and control, not because it has been shown to cut pain.

## 8. Implications for a local QI project

| What the literature supports | What it does not support |
| --- | --- |
| Offering PCS as an option to patients who want sedation | Using PCS routinely to reduce intraoperative pain |
| Copying the PACSPI 2 pump recipe in an anaesthetic setting | Unmonitored or non-anaesthesia PCS with propofol–alfentanil |
| Expecting better operating conditions and less rescue sedation | Expecting shorter lists or higher satisfaction scores |
| Expecting occasional bradypnoea and rare hypoxia | Treating PCS as “safer than LA” |
| Measuring experience, choice, fidelity, and safety | Using pain NRS as the main success metric |

Knowledge gaps that a local QI project can address, even if it cannot close them as research: how to identify who will benefit *before* the procedure; how a decision aid performs in real lists; whether the PACSPI safety profile holds outside a trial; and what the intervention costs in local kronor and minutes.

## 9. Bottom line

PCS with small propofol–alfentanil boluses is feasible, generally well tolerated, and preferred by many patients for short procedures. For SVP implantation specifically, a well-conducted Swedish RCT shows that it does not reduce pain compared with LA alone, but it improves operating conditions, nearly eliminates unplanned rescue sedation, and can be offered to those who want to stay in control of their own sedation. That is a sufficient evidence base for a carefully monitored local implementation project — and a clear warning not to sell the change as a pain intervention.
